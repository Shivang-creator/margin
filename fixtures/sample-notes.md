# Mitochondria and cellular respiration (Bio 101, week 4)

## What a mitochondrion is

The word mitochondrion was coined by Carl Benda in 1898. It comes from the Greek for "thread" and "granule".

The nickname "powerhouse of the cell" is much later. Philip Siekevitz used it as the title of a Scientific American article in 1957.

A typical mitochondrion has a cross-section of about 0.75 to 3 square micrometres. Muscle cells carry far more of them than most other cells.

Mature mammalian red blood cells have no mitochondria at all. They run on glycolysis alone.

## Structure (learn the four compartments)

- Outer membrane: smooth, with porins that let small molecules through.
- Intermembrane space: the gap between the two membranes. Protons get pumped in here.
- Inner membrane: folded into cristae, which increase its surface area. The electron transport chain and ATP synthase sit in this membrane.
- Matrix: the space inside the inner membrane. The citric acid cycle runs here. Mitochondrial DNA and ribosomes live here too.

Definition: cristae are the folds of the inner membrane. More folds means more membrane, which means more room for ATP synthase.

## Mitochondrial DNA

Mitochondria carry their own small circular genome. The human one is 16,569 base pairs long.

It encodes 37 genes. Thirteen are for subunits of the respiratory complexes, 22 are for tRNAs and two are for rRNAs.

## The three stages of aerobic respiration

Definition: aerobic respiration is the breakdown of glucose to carbon dioxide and water using oxygen, with the energy captured as ATP.

### 1. Glycolysis (cytoplasm)

Glucose is split into two pyruvate molecules. Two ATP are spent in the first half and four are made in the second half. Along the way, two NAD+ are reduced to NADH.

Net gain from glycolysis: 2 ATP per glucose.

### 2. Pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle (matrix)

Pyruvate enters the matrix and is converted to acetyl-CoA, releasing CO2. Acetyl-CoA (two carbons) joins oxaloacetate (four carbons) to form citrate, which has six carbons. The cycle then strips the carbons back off as CO2.

Each turn of the cycle produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH2 and 1 GTP (which can be converted to ATP). One glucose gives two turns, so the totals per glucose are 6 NADH, 2 FADH2 and 2 ATP.

### 3. Electron transport chain and chemiosmosis (inner membrane)

NADH and FADH2 hand their electrons to the electron transport chain. As electrons move down the chain, the complexes pump protons out of the matrix into the intermembrane space. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor and is reduced to water.

Definition: chemiosmosis is the use of that proton gradient to make ATP. Protons flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase, and the flow drives the enzyme to add a phosphate to ADP.

The chemiosmotic hypothesis was proposed in 1961. Peter Mitchell was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1978 for it.

The synthase itself makes 1 ATP for every 3 protons that pass through it. Counting the cost of moving ADP and phosphate into the matrix, the real figure is closer to 4 protons per ATP.

Rule of thumb: 1 NADH is worth about 2.5 ATP and 1 FADH2 is worth about 1.5 ATP. FADH2 is worth less because it skips complex I and so pumps fewer protons.

## How much ATP per glucose?

Older textbooks say 38 ATP per glucose: 2 from glycolysis, 2 from the citric acid cycle, and about 34 from the electron transport chain.

That is a theoretical maximum. Leaky membranes and the cost of transport into the matrix bring it down. The current estimate is 30 or 32 ATP per glucose, depending on which shuttle carries the glycolysis NADH into the mitochondrion.

Fermentation stays at the glycolysis figure, because pyruvate never reaches the mitochondrion. That's why aerobic respiration is up to 15 times more efficient than anaerobic.

## Worked example: counting the ATP for one glucose

1. Glycolysis: 2 ATP directly, plus 2 NADH.
2. Pyruvate oxidation: 2 NADH.
3. Citric acid cycle: 2 ATP directly, plus 6 NADH and 2 FADH2.
4. Carriers in total: 10 NADH and 2 FADH2.
5. Convert: 10 NADH times 2.5 gives 25 ATP; 2 FADH2 times 1.5 gives 3 ATP.
6. Add the 4 direct ATP: 25 + 3 + 4 = 32 ATP.

If the glycolysis NADH enters through the glycerol phosphate shuttle it counts as FADH2 instead, so those two carriers give 3 ATP rather than 5, and the total drops to 30. That's where "30 or 32" comes from.

## Things I keep getting wrong

- Glycolysis is in the cytoplasm, not the mitochondrion.
- The citric acid cycle makes almost no ATP by itself. Its job is to load NADH and FADH2.
- Oxygen is used at the very end, by complex IV, not in the citric acid cycle.
- Protons are pumped OUT of the matrix and flow back IN through ATP synthase.
